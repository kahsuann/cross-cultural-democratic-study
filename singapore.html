Singapore, A Civil Servant's Perspective

My name is Wei Ling. I'm 24 this year, and I work in the public service. Today is just another weekday morning. I step off the bus in front of Raffles Place MRT, swept along by the familiar rush of office workers. The MRT station is brightly lit, orderly, and efficient. Everything runs on time, like it always does. I stumble along the crowd, half-awake, half-thinking about the meetings ahead.

As I pass the voting billboard displayed near the station exit, it showed instructions on how to mark your ballot properly for GE2025 and I suddenly remember the first time I voted at 21. There were no crowds gathered to chant slogans, no dramatic speeches, no confrontations with police. Just neat lines at a school hall, fans whirring overhead, volunteers in white gloves guiding us to the booths. It felt calm, civil, almost understated. But I remember being struck by the same thought that returns now - most Singaporeans participate in democracy quietly, almost invisibly.

On the walk to my office, I reflect on how civic engagement here looks nothing like the protest footage we see from other countries. We do not take to the streets in thousands, holding umbrellas or placards. Protest is legal only in specific spaces like Speakers' Corner, and most people don't think of it as their first instinct for political expression. Instead, our politics happens in other ways, like at Meet-the-People sessions, in feedback forms, in government websites, in letters to MPs. It is governance through conversations, not confrontation.

I used to think this meant Singaporeans were simply apolitical, too busy or too comfortable to care. But working in the public service changed that. I started seeing how much people do engage, just not loudly. Residents who queue for hours to speak to their MP about a neighbour dispute or healthcare bill. Volunteers spending evenings tutoring children in rental blocks. Civil servants drafting policy papers based on consultation feedback from the ground. These small, unseen acts are political too.
