Hong Kong, A (Former) Protestor's Perspective

My name is Charlie, and I'm 30 years old this year. It's just another workday as I squeeze past the crowd at the exit of Admiralty MRT station only to be greeted by a sudden downpour. I then fish my umbrella from my backpack and spring it open before making my usual way to the office which, just a 11-minute walk away.

Somehow, the sight of people holding umbrellas ahead of me reminds me of the 2014 Umbrella Movement - I was only a sophomore at the Chinese University of Hong Kong back then. China's National People's Congress Standing Committee had just announced that all Hong Kong voters could vote for their Chief Executive but only for candidates pre-approved by a 1,200-member Election Committee. I was a member of the Hong Kong Federation of Students which disapproved of the announcement as it meant that there was no true universal suffrage.

As I walk past Tamar Park, I remember how I participated in the class boycotts and sit-ins with my fellow schoolmates. At the age of 19, I sat on the asphalt chanting in solidarity with fellow students: "we want real universal suffrage!". Being shoulder-to-shoulder with thousands of others made me feel like a part of a collective that could have our voices truly heard.

Just as I am about to cross the road, a sharp honk snaps me out of my thought. I step back automatically, allowing a taxi to surge past in a spray of dirty water. What a stark difference from 2014, I think to myself, when we could sit in the middle of this very road without flinching at the sound of a horn, and without the same constant fear of intimidation and threats from the police.

As the traffic light turned green, I hurry across and make my way up the familiar flight of stairs to the overhead bridge. From here, I can see the curve of Harcourt Road below me jammed with cars and buses. 11 years ago, this same spot looked completely different. I was among the protestors with linked arms trying to get the government to hear us out. It was a peaceful protest but the police started using tear gas and pepper spray on us to try and get us to disperse, resulting in the crowd raising umbrellas to shield ourselves, cementing the namesake of the movement.

On the other side of the bridge, I descend towards the entrance that connects to my office tower. This corridor once held a small Lennon Wall with a patchwork of post-its that people put up to show their support for fellow Hongkongers. Eventually, this was also cleaned up and the wall is now bare. At 19, I thought being a good citizen meant that I had to show up and lend my voice to what I believe in. But now at 30, I've lost hope. Why does it matter how I vote anyway, when the only candidates that can run are the pre-selected so-called patriots? And what's the point of trying to speak up since national security law has been imposed, and the ones who dared had to flee in fear of their freedom to do so?

Someone slams into my shoulder from behind and my umbrella slips from my fingers, clattering onto the wet floor. I can't help but feel this is a small nudge from the universe reminding me to leave that version of me behind and move on. I smile to myself wryly at how a typical 11-minute walk to the office somehow becomes a flashback to 11 years ago. As I join the queue for the lifts, one question lingers at the back of my head: In today's Hong Kong where protest and dissent feel increasingly risky and constrained, what does it really mean to be a good citizen?
