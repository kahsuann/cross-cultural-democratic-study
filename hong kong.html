Hong Kong

It is just another workday. I squeezed past the crowd at the exit of Admiralty MRT station only to be greeted by a sudden downpour. I then fished my umbrella from my backpack and sprang it open before making my usual way to the office which was just a ten-minute walk away.

Somehow, the sight of people holding umbrellas ahead of me reminded me of the 2014 Umbrella Movement* - I was only a sophomore at the Chinese University of Hong Kong back then. China's National People's Congress Standing Committee had just announced that all Hong Kong voters could vote for their Chief Executive but only for candidates pre-approved by a 1,200-member Election Committee. I was a member of the Hong Kong Federation of Students which disapproved of the announcement as it meant that there was no true universal suffrage.

As I walked past Tamar Park, I remembered how I participated in the class boycotts and sit-ins with my fellow schoolmates*. At the age of 19, I sat on the asphalt chanting in solidarity with fellow students: “we want real universal suffrage!”. Being shoulder-to-shoulder with thousands of others made me feel like a part of a collective that can have our voices truly heard and not easily ignored by the government.

Just as I am about to cross the road, a sharp honk snaps me out of my thought. I step back automatically, allowing a taxi to surge past in a spray of dirty water. What a stark difference from 2014, I thought to myself, when we could sit in the middle of this very road without flinching at the sound of a horn, and without the same constant fear of intimidation and threats from the police.

As the traffic light turned green, I hurried across and made my way up the familiar flight of stairs to the overhead bridge. From here, I could see the curve of Harcourt Road below me jammed with cars and buses. 11 years ago, this same spot looked completely different. I was among the protestors with linked arms trying to get the government to hear us out. It was a peaceful protest but the police started using tear gas and pepper spray on us to try and get us to disperse, resulting in the crowd raising umbrellas to shield ourselves which cemented the namesake of the movement.

On the other side of the bridge, I descend towards the entrance that connects to my office tower. This corridor once held a small Lennon Wall with a patchwork of post-its that people put up to show their support for fellow Hongkongers*. Eventually, this was also cleaned up and the wall is now bare. At 19, I thought being a good citizen meant that I had to show up and lend my voice to what I believe in. But now at 30, my definition seems to have shifted. It now means paying my taxes on time, staying out of trouble and voting without expecting the ballot to change much. The space for protests has shrunk and I no longer dare to speak up, especially after seeing how notable activists were subdued or forced to live in self-exile.

Someone slams into my shoulder from behind and my umbrella slips from my fingers, clattering onto the wet floor. I could not help but to feel this was a small nudge from the universe reminding me to leave that version of me behind and keep moving on. I chuckled as a typical 11-minute walk to the office somehow became a flashback to 11 years ago. As I join the queue for the lifts, one question lingers at the back of my head: In today’s Hong Kong where protest and dissent feel increasingly risky and constrained, what does it really mean to be a good citizen now?
